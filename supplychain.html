Supply Chain Management

Your Supply Chain Knows More Than You Think

PrimeChain · Logistics & Supply Chain Management

A supply chain is more than a collection of departments. It is a connected system in which the performance of one activity influences what happens before and after it.

A late delivery may appear to be a transport problem. A stockout may appear to be a warehouse problem. A delayed production order may appear to be a manufacturing problem.

But the visible problem is not necessarily where the underlying problem began.

Understanding this distinction is one of the most valuable capabilities in supply chain management.

The supply chain is an interconnected system

Consider the activities involved in fulfilling a customer order.

Materials or products must be sourced, received, stored, made available, processed where necessary, picked, dispatched and delivered.

Depending on the business, this may involve procurement, suppliers, receiving, warehousing, inventory management, production, transport, distribution and customer service.

These activities are not always sequential. Some happen simultaneously, while returns, information updates and payments move in different directions.

The important point is that each function depends on information and decisions made elsewhere.

A problem experienced in one part of the supply chain may have originated somewhere else.

To understand performance, management must examine how the entire process works together rather than judging each department in isolation.

Three flows that shape supply chain performance

Supply chains can be understood through three closely related flows.

1. The flow of materials and products

This includes raw materials, components, work-in-progress, finished goods, equipment and returned items.

It concerns what moves, where it moves, how much moves and when it becomes available.

2. The flow of information

Purchase orders, delivery notes, inventory records, production schedules, picking instructions, dispatch confirmations and customer orders communicate what should happen and what has actually happened.

Information must be accurate, timely and accessible to the people who depend on it.

3. The flow of money

Supplier payments, inventory holding costs, transport charges, production costs, customer invoices and collections influence the financial performance of the chain.

A transaction can appear operationally complete while its financial reconciliation is still outstanding.

Strong supply chain management considers the relationship between all three flows.

Receiving and inventory: where small errors spread

Consider an illustrative distribution business.

A supplier delivers 1,000 units. The receiving document records all 1,000 as received.

During inspection, however, 30 units are found to be damaged and placed on quality hold.

If the system continues showing all 1,000 units as available for sale, the inventory record overstates what can actually be fulfilled.

The business then receives an order for 990 units.

1,000 units received - 30 units on hold = 970 units potentially available.

Assuming no other reservations or restrictions, there is a shortfall of 20 units against the new order.

The warehouse may discover this when the order is picked. Distribution may then miss the planned dispatch.

The customer experiences a fulfilment problem, even though the underlying information error began at receiving and inventory control.

These quantities are illustrative, not results from a PrimeChain client engagement.

The lesson is not that receiving staff are necessarily responsible for every shortage. It is that stock status, physical quantities and system availability must be reconciled and communicated accurately.

Warehousing: more than storing goods

Warehousing connects the availability of stock with the demands placed on the operation.

Receiving accuracy, put-away discipline, storage locations, picking methods and dispatch verification all influence performance.

An item may physically exist in the warehouse but still be unavailable because it is damaged, reserved, incorrectly located or awaiting inspection.

That is why physical stock, recorded stock and available-to-promise inventory are not necessarily the same thing.

Useful management questions include:

  • Do inventory records reflect actual stock?
  • How quickly do received goods become available?
  • Which stock movements require reconciliation?
  • How often do picking exceptions occur?
  • Are damaged, reserved and returned items clearly distinguished?

Answers to these questions help management identify whether the issue concerns inventory accuracy, process discipline, capacity or information flow.

Production: planning depends on reliable inputs

In a production environment, materials, capacity and scheduling must work together.

A production plan may assume that a required component is available. If the inventory record is incorrect, the plan may be impossible to execute as scheduled.

Alternatively, the materials may be available, but equipment downtime, labour capacity, quality problems or sequencing constraints may delay output.

The relevant information must therefore connect inventory availability, production requirements, actual output and changes to the schedule.

A useful question is not only whether production met its target, but why any difference between planned and actual output occurred.

Distribution: the final outcome reflects the chain

Distribution is often where earlier operational problems become visible to the customer.

Products may arrive late because stock was not available, orders were released too late, loading took longer than planned or transport capacity was insufficient.

Looking only at vehicle departure and arrival times may therefore miss the true source of poor delivery performance.

For transport operators, another important control is reconciliation between what was scheduled, loaded, transported and delivered.

Consider bulk haulage involving weighbridge records at loading and receiving points.

A quantity difference requires investigation, but it is not automatically proof of theft or financial loss.

Measurement methods, tare weights, moisture, documentation errors, agreed tolerances and equipment calibration may all require consideration, depending on the commodity and operating conditions.

The aim is to identify and resolve exceptions using reliable evidence rather than assumptions.

Supply chain visibility is not simply a dashboard

A dashboard can present useful information, but the quality of that information depends on the processes and records behind it.

If receiving quantities are incorrect, inventory statuses are outdated or delivery confirmations are missing, a dashboard may simply display an incomplete version of reality.

Operational visibility requires appropriate information capture, clear responsibilities, connected records and processes for resolving discrepancies.

Technology becomes valuable when it helps these elements work together.

The same principles apply beyond traditional logistics

Consider a contractor managing electricity meter installations.

Equipment is received into inventory, issued to technicians, installed at sites and reconciled against completed work.

Old equipment may need to be returned, and field evidence may need supervisor approval before the job is considered fully closed.

If inventory records, technician allocations and work orders are disconnected, management may struggle to establish who holds which equipment and which installations have been properly documented.

Although this differs from a conventional distribution operation, the same principles of material flow, custody, verification and information accuracy apply.

Five steps to assess your own supply chain

1. Follow one customer order or operational requirement

Trace it backwards and forwards through the relevant activities, from initial demand and sourcing to fulfilment and confirmation.

Include the supporting documents, stock movements and important decisions.

2. Identify the dependencies

Determine which department depends on which information from another department.

For example, what must procurement know before placing an order, and what must the warehouse confirm before dispatch?

3. Reconcile planned and actual activity

Compare orders with receipts, inventory records with physical stock, production plans with actual output, and dispatch records with delivery evidence.

Investigate differences before assuming their cause.

4. Assign ownership of exceptions

Decide who investigates missing information, stock discrepancies, delayed orders and other operational exceptions.

Give each issue a status, responsible person and clear resolution process.

5. Measure improvement over time

Establish a baseline, make targeted changes and compare performance using consistent definitions and reporting periods.

Start with a manageable number of measures rather than collecting data without a purpose.

Measures that can reveal supply chain performance

Depending on the business, useful performance indicators may include:

  • Inventory record accuracy: How closely recorded stock agrees with verified physical quantities.
  • Order fulfilment performance: The proportion of orders fulfilled according to the agreed quantity and delivery requirements.
  • Order cycle time: The time between receiving an order and completing fulfilment.
  • Stockout frequency: How often required products or materials are unavailable when needed.
  • Production schedule adherence: How closely actual production follows the agreed production plan.
  • Delivery exceptions: The frequency and causes of shortages, delays, missing documentation or other delivery discrepancies.

Each measure should have a clear definition, a reliable source and a person responsible for reviewing the results.

Measurements become most valuable when management can use them to investigate causes and make operational improvements.

What your supply chain may be telling you

A late delivery, an inaccurate stock record or an interrupted production schedule should not be viewed only as an isolated departmental failure.

Each may be a signal of a wider process, capacity, planning or information problem.

Supply chain management requires understanding how activities connect, how constraints move through the system and how decisions in one function affect the others.

The real opportunity is not simply to see more information. It is to understand what the information means across the entire operation.

Begin with one order, one movement or one recurring exception.

Follow it through the chain. Identify the dependencies. Examine the records. Understand the root cause.

That is where operational visibility becomes useful management intelligence.

Your supply chain may be telling you more than you think. The advantage comes from knowing how to interpret its signals.
