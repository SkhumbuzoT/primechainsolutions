The R200,000 Fuel Leak Nobody Could See

A bulk logistics operator had just secured its biggest contract to date. 20,000 tonnes of RB1 coal. Forty contracted trucks. Hundreds of loads moving from Mpumalanga to Durban.

On paper, it was the kind of contract that should have made the year. Instead, the operation was slowly bleeding margin through a cost everyone had learned to accept.

Fuel.

The numbers were getting bigger. The answers weren't.

Fuel invoices arrived every week. Large numbers. Sometimes larger than expected.

The team knew fuel was expensive. They knew prices were moving. They knew forty trucks doing hundreds of loads would consume a lot of diesel.

So the invoices were paid.

Fuel was treated as:

“Just a cost of doing business.”

But on a contract where margins were measured in single digits, that assumption was becoming dangerous.

The problem wasn't that the business couldn't see its fuel spend. It could.

The problem was that it couldn't see what was driving it.

The problem wasn't the invoice

The operation could tell you:

How much fuel was purchased.

It couldn't reliably tell you:

  • Which truck consumed it.
  • Which loads it supported.
  • Which route it was associated with.
  • Whether the consumption was reasonable.
  • Which vehicles were consistently above expectation.
  • Whether a contractor's performance was deteriorating.

The fuel data and the operational data existed. They simply weren't connected.

So an invoice could be questioned at an accounting level, but not at an operational level.

There was no practical way to say:

“This truck consumed more fuel than its operating pattern should require.”

We started with the work, not the fuel

Rather than building another report of fuel expenditure, we worked backwards from the operation.

A truck performs a load. That load follows a route. The truck travels a known distance. The operation has an expected consumption profile. Fuel is purchased along the way.

If those events can be connected, fuel stops being an invoice. It becomes an operational measurement.

The model became:

Truck → Load → Route → Distance → Expected Consumption → Actual Fuel → Variance

That changed the question completely.

Establishing what “normal” looks like

The first requirement was a baseline.

Not every truck should necessarily be judged against the same number. Vehicle type, operating conditions, route characteristics, load profile and historical performance all matter.

We therefore established an expected consumption profile against which actual fuel activity could be compared.

For each truck, the system could consider factors such as:

  • Expected fuel consumption
  • Distance travelled
  • Route
  • Loads completed
  • Tonnes moved
  • Fuel issued
  • Fuel price
  • Operating period

The objective was simple:

Know what fuel consumption should reasonably look like before deciding that actual consumption is abnormal.

Connecting fuel to the operation

The breakthrough came from linking records that had previously been viewed separately.

Fuel transactions were associated with the trucks consuming the fuel. Operational records established loads and routes. Distance and trip information provided the context.

The system could then begin constructing a picture of actual consumption.

Instead of:

R1.2 million fuel expense

management could see:

  • Truck 17 — above expected
  • Truck 23 — within expected range
  • Truck 31 — repeated variance
  • Route B — elevated consumption across multiple vehicles

That is a very different level of visibility.

From fuel spend to fuel variance

A fuel invoice tells you what you paid. A fuel-control system asks:

Was that consumption reasonable?

The important number is not simply the additional R12,200. It is the fact that the system can now ask:

Why?

  • Is the truck genuinely working harder?
  • Is the route different?
  • Is there excessive idling?
  • Is the vehicle mechanically inefficient?
  • Are there unusually high fuel transactions?
  • Is the fuel being associated with the correct vehicle and activity?
  • Or is there another operational explanation?

The system started looking for patterns

One abnormal transaction isn't necessarily a problem. Repeated abnormal behaviour is much more interesting.

So the analysis moved beyond individual fuel transactions. We looked for patterns across:

Truck

Which vehicles consistently consumed above expectation?

Route

Did certain routes produce higher-than-expected consumption?

Load

Did fuel consumption make sense relative to loads completed?

Time

Were there periods where fuel activity didn't align with operational activity?

Contractor

Were certain contracted vehicles consistently outside the expected range?

The objective wasn't to accuse. It was to identify where investigation was warranted.

That changed accountability

Before the system, a fuel invoice could be challenged only in aggregate. After the system, management could have a much more specific conversation.

Not:

“Fuel costs are too high.”

But:

“Truck 17 has consumed 11% above its expected fuel profile across the last 18 loads. Let's understand why.”

That changes the operational conversation.

  • A contractor can investigate the vehicle.
  • A fleet manager can investigate maintenance.
  • Operations can investigate routing and turnaround.
  • Management can investigate whether the commercial model still protects the contract margin.

The data creates a starting point for action.

The first major leak became visible

Once the fuel transactions were connected to the operational activity, the scale of the problem became difficult to ignore.

R200,000+

Potential fuel leakage / exposure identified across the vehicles analysed.

The significance wasn't simply the amount. It was the fact that the business had been paying for the fuel without having a reliable mechanism to determine where the excess consumption was occurring.

The leak wasn't hidden because the data didn't exist. It was hidden because the data wasn't connected.

From one investigation to a control system

The goal wasn't to find one problem and produce one report. The system was designed so that the same analysis could continue as the operation moved.

  • New fuel transactions could be compared against expected behaviour.
  • New loads could be linked to trucks and routes.
  • Exceptions could be surfaced.
  • Historical performance could be compared.
  • And management could monitor whether identified issues were isolated incidents or recurring patterns.

The solution therefore moved from:

Investigation

to

Continuous control.

What changed

Before

  • Fuel invoices viewed in aggregate
  • Spend visible
  • No per-truck baseline
  • Problems discovered at month-end
  • Difficult to challenge contractors
  • Fuel treated as a fixed operating cost
  • Data existed in separate places
  • Leakage difficult to explain

After

  • Fuel activity linked to operational activity
  • Consumption visible
  • Expected truck-level consumption
  • Exceptions surfaced through ongoing analysis
  • Evidence-based performance conversations
  • Fuel treated as a measurable operational variable
  • Data connected around the truck and load
  • Variance could be investigated

The outcome wasn't a fuel dashboard

The dashboard was only the interface. The real solution was the control logic underneath it.

  • Fuel transactions were connected to trucks.
  • Trucks were connected to operational activity.
  • Operational activity was connected to routes and loads.
  • Actual consumption was compared against expected behaviour.
  • And exceptions were surfaced for investigation.

That created something the business didn't have before:

A way to see where fuel performance was moving away from expectation while the operation was still running.

PrimeChain Perspective

Operational costs rarely announce themselves as problems. They arrive as invoices. They appear as small variances. They get absorbed into monthly totals.

And eventually someone says: “That's just the cost of running the business.”

But when margins are tight, “just a cost” can become the place where profitability disappears.

The answer isn't always more reporting. Sometimes the business already has all the data it needs. It just needs the right connections.

At PrimeChain, we connect operational records to the models and controls needed to understand what is actually happening.

Because the question isn't: “How much fuel did we buy?”
It's: “Which trucks consumed it, what work did they perform, what should they have consumed, and where is the difference?”

That is where operational data becomes operational intelligence.
